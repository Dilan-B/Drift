Force 1 · The reward loop
A slot machine in your pocket

Variable-ratio reinforcement. B.F. Skinner found that rewards delivered on an unpredictable schedule are the most compulsive of all — more than a predictable reward every time.

Pull to refresh. Maybe something great, maybe nothing. That "maybe" is the exact mechanism slot machines use — now on the screen you check 205 times a day.

Force 2 · Brain chemistry
Dopamine isn't the pleasure chemical
The myth

"Dopamine = pleasure. Likes make us feel good."

If it were really pleasure, we'd feel satisfied and stop. We don't.

What the research shows

Dopamine drives wanting — not liking.

Berridge & Robinson's work separates seeking from enjoying. Apps flood the 'seeking' system — so you keep chasing a hit you never quite land. That's the itch you can't scratch.

Force 3 · No brakes
Designed to have no natural stopping point
1

Infinite scroll

The page never ends. Older designs had page 2, page 3 — a natural 'shall I go on?' moment. That pause was deliberately removed.

2

Autoplay

The next video starts before you decide to watch it. The default is 'keep going,' and defaults win.

3

Streaks & badges

Manufactured obligations that punish you for stopping — quit and you 'lose' something.

The old way had stopping cues

An empty plate. The end of a chapter. The last page of the newspaper. A signal that said: you're done.

The feed removed all of them.

Force 4 · The hidden tax
Every glance costs more than the glance

Attention residue. Every time you switch — even a five-second glance at a notification — part of your mind stays stuck on the last thing. Sophie Leroy named this in 2009: your full attention doesn't arrive with you.

So a five-second check doesn't cost five seconds. UC Irvine's Gloria Mark clocked the average return to full focus at 23 minutes. Her latest work: our attention on any one screen now lasts about 47 seconds.

The body keeps the score
It follows you into the night
Blue light delays sleep

In a Harvard study (PNAS, 2015), reading on a screen before bed cut melatonin by 50%+ and pushed the body clock back ~1.5 hours — with groggier mornings after.

The bedtime doomscroll

The variable-reward loop is most powerful when you're tired and your guard is down — exactly when you meant to sleep.

Displacement

Every hour on the phone is an hour not spent sleeping, moving, or with people. Often it's the sleep and face-to-face time that lose.

The generation that grew up on it
The timing is hard to ignore

Teen mental health held broadly steady through the 2000s — then turned sharply downward around 2012–2013, just as smartphones and social media became near-universal. The rise in anxiety, depression and self-harm was steepest among teen girls.

Psychologist Jonathan Haidt (The Anxious Generation, 2024) calls it a "great rewiring of childhood." The science is still correlational and debated — but few dispute the pattern.

Drift · The Science of Screen Time14
Principle 1
Add friction to the bad, remove it from the good

Small speed bumps beat big intentions. A 15-second delay is often enough to break the automatic reach.

Grayscale mode

Draining the color makes the feed far less magnetic — the dopamine cues dim.

Delete, don't just close

Kill the app; use it in the browser. The extra step is the point.

Log out every time

Re-typing the password is a 15-second pause that lets the urge pass.

Hide the triggers

Off the home screen, into a folder on page 3. Out of sight, out of thumb.

Kill the badges

Turn off red notification dots — they manufacture urgency that isn't real.

Charge it in another room

Friction for the worst offender: the bedtime and first-thing scroll.

Drift · The Science of Screen Time15
Principle 2
Design defaults, because defaults win

You will not make a fresh, wise decision 96 times a day. So don't rely on deciding — set the default once, and let it carry you.

✓

Phone-free zones

The dinner table. The bedroom. The first 30 minutes awake. Make the rule about the place, not the moment.

✓

A basket by the door

A home for the phone when you walk in, so it isn't fused to your hand all evening.

✓

Analog alarm clock

Breaks the most damaging loop: phone last thing at night, first in the morning.

✓

Scheduled downtime

Use the phone's own tools to auto-lock distracting apps at set hours — a default you don't renegotiate.

Drift · The Science of Screen Time16
Principle 3
Commitment devices: bind your future self

A choice you make now, while you're clear-headed, that limits what tired-you can do later. Odysseus tying himself to the mast so he couldn't chase the sirens — same idea, modern problem.

App limits with teeth

Set a hard cap you can't casually override in the moment of weakness.

Stakes & promises

Tell someone your goal, or put money on it. Now quitting has a cost.

Earn-it systems

Make screen time contingent on something first — a walk, a task, real life.

Drift · The Science of Screen Time17
Principle 4
Replace the habit — don't just delete it

A phone habit fills a real need — boredom, stress, connection, a moment's rest. Remove it without a replacement and the vacuum pulls you right back.

Bored in a queue → A book or podcast queued and ready

Winding down at night → A bath, a stretch, a few pages of fiction

Craving connection → A text to a real friend, or a call

Need a mental break → A walk outside — five minutes resets more than a scroll

Drift · The Science of Screen Time18
Principle 5
Do it with other people

Willpower is a solo sport and it loses. Accountability borrows other people's — and turns a private struggle into a shared one.

✓

Phone stacks

At dinner, everyone's phone goes in a pile. First to reach for it does the dishes.

✓

A screen-time buddy

Share weekly numbers with a friend. Being seen changes behavior more than any setting.

✓

Family charging station

One spot where everyone's phone sleeps at night. The rule applies to parents too.

✓

Tell people your why

Saying the goal out loud makes backing out cost something social.
